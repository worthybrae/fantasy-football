A draft assistant that knew how each manager thinks could predict the picks ahead of yours. We had one measurement pointing that way: how far off market rank a manager picks carries from season to season, while which positions he favours does not.

That looked finished. It was half an answer, measured in the wrong window, and the half that was wrong turned out to be the interesting one.

## Two different questions

"Does last year's draft predict this year's" and "does what he has done so far today predict his next pick" are not the same measurement. A live assistant needs both — the first decides whether history is worth carrying in, the second decides whether the model should move as picks land.

A trait can pass one and fail the other, so every trait here is measured twice.

It is also easy to fool yourself across fourteen correlations. At that many, an |r| of 0.35 appears somewhere by luck about half the time. So every number below carries a family-wise p as well as its own: permute which manager each season's numbers belong to, and ask how often the best cell in the whole family beats this one. Where the two disagree, the family-wise one is the verdict.

## Weighting the picks that carry the signal

Late picks are constrained — by what is left, and by the slots still open — so they say less about taste than a first-round choice does. Truncating to rounds 1-5 is the crude version of that idea. Weighting every pick by {decay}^(round-1) is the better one: it discounts the late rounds without discarding them.

It moves the trait we already believed in. Reach goes from {across_reach_truncated} truncated to **{across_reach}** decayed, and that survives correction at p = {across_reach_pfam}.

It does nothing for the two habits we had never tested — whether a manager buys the projection or the track record, and whether he buys weekly reliability.

## The window was wrong for position

Positional preference was measured over rounds 1-5 and found to be nothing. But which position a manager takes early is dictated by the board. How many tight ends he walks away with is a choice — and it is measured over the whole draft, not the first third of it.

## Inside a single draft, the same trait runs backwards

Same traits, now asking whether a manager's early rounds predict his late ones, with the round-bucket average removed so that shared late-round drift is not read as eight managers sharing one habit.

::signed caption="do his early rounds predict his late rounds? ({within_n_reach} manager-drafts)"
QB|{within_pos_QB}

TE|{within_pos_TE}
reaches vs waits|{within_reach}
::

Every position goes NEGATIVE, and tight end hardest at {within_pos_TE}. A manager who has taken his tight ends does not take more.

That is not a contradiction of the section above, it is the mechanism behind it. A manager has a roster shape he is aiming at. Within a draft he balances toward it, which makes early and late run opposite. Across seasons the target itself barely moves. Both numbers are the same fact seen from two directions.

It also matters that the sign is negative rather than positive: a model treating positional preference as a taste to be indulged would push its predictions the wrong way. The thing to predict is not "he likes tight ends", it is "he is going to end up with two".

Meanwhile reach, the trait with the real season-to-season prior, comes in at {within_reach} within a draft — family-wise p = {within_reach_pfam}. What is real about reach is the prior, not the update.

## What we changed

The model keeps two personal coefficients and pools everything else.

- **Reach**, decayed by round rather than truncated at five.
- **Roster shape**, as a target count per position, which is where the strongest signal in this experiment actually lives.

Growth and steadiness are dropped. They are indistinguishable from zero in all four of their numbers, and the one that briefly was not is the reason this experiment reports family-wise p at all.

The honest caveat is power. {across_n_reach} owner-season pairs is small; reach and tight end clearing the bar shows that effects of THAT size are detectable, not that the others are absent. Two of these traits may be real and simply invisible at {n_picks} picks across {n_managers} managers.

Which argues for collecting more drafts rather than for modelling harder.
