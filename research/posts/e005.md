ESPN publishes a projected stat line for every player: carries, rushing yards, targets, receptions, receiving yards. Our board consumes one number from it — the fantasy total — and ignores the five forecasts underneath.

Those five are not equally good, and the weakest is not the one you would guess.

Yardage is forecast well. **Receptions are ESPN's weakest stat at {espn_h2h_receptions}** — which matters more than it looks, because in a PPR league a reception is a point. The thing they predict worst is the thing our scoring rewards most.

## Is that just because projection is hard?

The test that matters is not whether ESPN is accurate in the abstract, but whether they beat the dumbest available alternative: assume a player repeats last season. Same players, same rows, so neither side gets an easier sample.

So ESPN is genuinely doing work. But look at the size of it: their edge over a baseline with no model at all is between {closest_gap} and {widest_gap}. They are better than doing nothing, and not by a great deal.

Where they earn it most is **{widest_stat}**, which is where a projection has to know something a stat sheet cannot tell you — who the team signed, who is hurt, how a backfield will be split. And they earn it completely on rookies, where the baseline has nothing to say at all: ESPN still forecasts a rookie's receiving yards at {espn_rookie_rec_yds} across {n_rookie_rows} of them.

## What this means for building our own

Two things, pointing in different directions.

Beating ESPN is not a formality. They are ahead of the naive baseline on every stat, they handle rookies, and any model of ours has to clear a bar we now know the height of.

But the bar is not high, and it is lowest exactly where PPR scoring is decided. {closest_gap} to {widest_gap} above "repeat last year" is a target a genuinely informed model should reach — and receptions, at {espn_h2h_receptions}, is the softest number on the board.

That is where we start.
