Strength of schedule: better model, same answer

Can we predict defensive strength better than last season's numbers do -- and if we can, does it change any draft decision?

We improved our strength-of-schedule model. Then we found out it barely matters.

We made it better

Today the board uses one input: what each defense gave up last season. Betting markets should beat that, because a line prices a team as it is now. We can read about 6 games per team from lines posted before a snap.

Opening lines are nearly as good as closing lines — the market's August read is most of its final read, which is what makes it usable at a draft. And it helps most against the pass, where last season is nearly useless.

Blending beats either alone, and the optimum is a plateau rather than a knife edge, so the exact weight hardly matters.

It didn't matter

Then we asked what a perfect schedule number would buy. Not our model — perfect hindsight.

The ceilingCheating-level knowledge of every defense improves a player projection by 0.0035 of R2. Our better model captures a fraction of that.

The reason is structural. Facing a defense one standard deviation softer really is worth 1.18 points a game. But players barely differ in the schedules they get: you face seventeen opponents, and averaging seventeen draws collapses the spread.

Soft season versus hard season is about 10.6 points across a year — a few percent of a starter's total.

What we changed

We took the better model, since it is free now that it is measured. We did not give it more weight. If anything the finding argues the other way: `schedule` is 10% of our composite, and it is pricing something worth a few percent of a season.

Season-long schedule averages out. Weeks 15 to 17 do not — three games instead of seventeen, and they decide most leagues. That is where we look next.
