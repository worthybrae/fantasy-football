Every serious projection system is built bottom-up: forecast team volume, forecast the player's share of it, forecast games played, multiply. The justification is always that the parts are steadier than the whole. Over {n_wr} receiver, {n_rb} back and {n_te} tight end seasons, they are not.

## Availability is the leak

> The weak link
Games played repeat at {r_games} across {n_games} skill-position seasons at a {move_min_games}-game floor. Nothing else in the decomposition is that noisy.

It surfaces where it should. Every season total is less predictable than the rate it was built from, and the difference is availability.

Backs take the worst of it: {rb_same_ppg} staying put against {rb_moved_ppg} after a move, {rb_same_total} against {rb_moved_total} on season totals. A new team is a new denominator and a new share, something only a decomposed model can write down.

Pooled across all positions the same split reads {pooled_same_ppg} against {pooled_moved_ppg} on points per game and {pooled_same_total} against {pooled_moved_total} on totals. Those are far larger than the per-position numbers above, and nothing got easier: pooling mixes kickers with quarterbacks, which widens the spread of talent and lifts any correlation measured inside it. Read them against each other and nothing else here.

## What we take from it

Decomposition does not earn its keep by being more predictable. It earns it by saying what a top-down model cannot: this receiver signed elsewhere, this back's line left, this rookie has no season to regress. Persistence is what you measure when nothing changed, and change is most of why anyone projects at all.

So we project rates, keep games played as its own uncertainty instead of hiding it in a total, and stop selling decomposition on a stability it does not have.
