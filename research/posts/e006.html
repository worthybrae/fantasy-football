Owner tendencies: which ones persist, and on which axis

A manager's habits should help predict his next pick -- but which habits, and does history predict them or does the draft in front of us?

A draft assistant that knew how each manager thinks could predict the picks ahead of yours. We had one measurement pointing that way: how far off market rank a manager picks carries from season to season, while which positions he favours does not.

That looked finished. It was half an answer, measured in the wrong window, and the half that was wrong turned out to be the interesting one.

Two different questions

"Does last year's draft predict this year's" and "does what he has done so far today predict his next pick" are not the same measurement. A live assistant needs both — the first decides whether history is worth carrying in, the second decides whether the model should move as picks land.

A trait can pass one and fail the other, so every trait here is measured twice.

It is also easy to fool yourself across fourteen correlations. At that many, an |r| of 0.35 appears somewhere by luck about half the time. So every number below carries a family-wise p as well as its own: permute which manager each season's numbers belong to, and ask how often the best cell in the whole family beats this one. Where the two disagree, the family-wise one is the verdict.

Weighting the picks that carry the signal

Late picks are constrained — by what is left, and by the slots still open — so they say less about taste than a first-round choice does. Truncating to rounds 1-5 is the crude version of that idea. Weighting every pick by 0.8^(round-1) is the better one: it discounts the late rounds without discarding them.

It moves the trait we already believed in. Reach goes from 0.408 truncated to **0.516** decayed, and that survives correction at p = 0.015.

It does nothing for the two habits we had never tested — whether a manager buys the projection or the track record, and whether he buys weekly reliability.

The one that got awaySteadiness reads -0.35 at p = 0.027, which looks like a finding. Its family-wise p is 0.402. It is the best of fourteen tries, and that is all it is.

The window was wrong for position

Positional preference was measured over rounds 1-5 and found to be nothing. But which position a manager takes early is dictated by the board. How many tight ends he walks away with is a choice — and it is measured over the whole draft, not the first third of it.

**Tight end persists at 0.692**, family-wise p = 0. Leaving any single manager out leaves it between 0.6 and 0.79. The raw counts are close to deterministic: two managers have taken exactly one tight end in each of 6 seasons, and a third has taken exactly two every time.

Receivers and backs point the same way at 0.428 and 0.374, and neither clears the family-wise bar on this much history.

Inside a single draft, the same trait runs backwards

Same traits, now asking whether a manager's early rounds predict his late ones, with the round-bucket average removed so that shared late-round drift is not read as eight managers sharing one habit.

Every position goes NEGATIVE, and tight end hardest at -0.708. A manager who has taken his tight ends does not take more.

That is not a contradiction of the section above, it is the mechanism behind it. A manager has a roster shape he is aiming at. Within a draft he balances toward it, which makes early and late run opposite. Across seasons the target itself barely moves. Both numbers are the same fact seen from two directions.

It also matters that the sign is negative rather than positive: a model treating positional preference as a taste to be indulged would push its predictions the wrong way. The thing to predict is not "he likes tight ends", it is "he is going to end up with two".

Meanwhile reach, the trait with the real season-to-season prior, comes in at 0.228 within a draft — family-wise p = 1. What is real about reach is the prior, not the update.

The one we could not measure

Whether a manager buys upside or proven production is the habit people most expect to be real, so it got 6 different shapes rather than one: how many young players he ends up with, how many rookies, how much draft capital he spends on youth, the same restricted to the rounds where he is really choosing, and -- the one that should have worked -- each pick's age and experience measured against the players ranked ALONGSIDE it.

That last shape exists because a manager's average draftee age is mostly a fact about who fell to him. Comparing a pick to its ADP neighbours asks the question the average cannot: offered a tier, did he take the younger man?

None of them moved. The best of the six is exp_peer at 0.188, family-wise p = 1.

Not measured, rather than measured as zeroTo confirm a correlation of 0.188 takes 221 owner-season pairs. We have 40.

That number explains the whole experiment, and it is worth reading against the traits that did survive. Tight end at 0.692 needs 14 pairs, and we have 40. Reach needs 28. Receiver share, at 0.428, needs 41 -- one more than we have, which is exactly why it sits on the boundary instead of landing on one side of it.

Nothing anomalous happened here. The habits that showed up showed up because they are large, not because they are the only real ones. A youth preference of the size hinted at would need roughly four leagues with this much history, or eleven with three seasons each.

What we changed

The model keeps two personal coefficients and pools everything else.

  • **Reach**, decayed by round rather than truncated at five.
  • **Roster shape**, as a target count per position, which is where the strongest signal in this experiment actually lives.

Growth and steadiness are dropped. They are indistinguishable from zero in all four of their numbers, and the one that briefly was not is the reason this experiment reports family-wise p at all.

Growth and steadiness are dropped, and so is the youth trait -- but dropped as UNMEASURED, not as disproven. At 712 picks across 8 managers and 6 seasons, an effect of the size they hint at is invisible by construction.

Which argues for collecting more drafts rather than for modelling harder.
