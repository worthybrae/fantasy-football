A draft assistant that knew how each manager thinks could predict the picks in front of yours. We already had one measurement pointing that way: how far off market rank a manager picks carries from season to season, while which positions he favours does not.

That looked like a finished answer. It was half of one.

## Two different questions

"Does last year's draft predict this year's" and "does what he has done so far today predict his next pick" are not the same measurement, and a live assistant needs both — the first decides whether history is worth carrying in, the second decides whether the model should move as picks land.

A trait can pass one and fail the other. Position is the obvious case: dead as a season-to-season habit, yet plainly live inside a draft, because nobody takes a third quarterback.

So every trait gets measured twice. Two of them are habits we had never tested — does he buy the projection or the track record, and does he buy weekly reliability — scored as of the draft, using the projection published that season and only the actuals available before it.

## Inside a single draft

Same traits, now asking whether a manager's early rounds predict his late ones. The round-bucket average is removed first — reaches grow late for everyone as the board thins, and that shared drift would otherwise read as eight managers sharing one personal trait.

That is roster balance, not taste, and the sign is what matters: a model treating positional preference as a persistent habit would push its predictions in exactly the wrong direction. Our `need` feature already prices the real effect.

Meanwhile reaching, the one trait with a real season-to-season prior, comes in at {within_reach} within a draft — p = {within_p_reach}, which does not clear the bar. What is real is the prior, not the update.

## What we changed

The four-trait, continuously-updating owner profile does not survive contact with its own measurement. Growth and steadiness are indistinguishable from zero on both axes, in all four numbers. Building per-owner coefficients for them would add parameters chasing an effect we have now looked for and failed to find.

So the model keeps one personal coefficient — reach — and pools the rest.

The honest caveat is power. {across_n_reach} owner-season pairs is small, and reach clearing the bar shows only that an effect of THAT size is detectable; a genuine correlation of 0.25 would need roughly three times this history to see. Two of these traits may be real and simply invisible at this sample.

Which is the argument for collecting more drafts rather than for modelling harder. {n_picks} picks across {n_managers} managers and {n_seasons} seasons is what we have; the answer to an underpowered measurement is more of it.
